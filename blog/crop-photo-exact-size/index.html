How to Crop a Photo to the Exact Size You Need

A passport photo service, a print lab, or an ID badge system asks for a photo at an exact size — not "about the right shape," an exact number of pixels or inches. Here's why that distinction matters and how to hit it precisely.

Aspect ratio vs exact dimensions — not the same thing

Cropping to an aspect ratio means keeping a proportion, like 1:1 for a square or 4:3 for a standard photo — the crop can come out at any actual size as long as the ratio holds. Cropping to an exact size means the output has to be a specific pixel count or physical dimension, like exactly 600×600 pixels or exactly 2×2 inches at 300 DPI. A crop can be perfectly square and still be the wrong exact size if you needed 600×600 and ended up with 800×800. Most casual cropping only cares about the ratio; the situations where exact size actually matters are more specific than people expect.

Where exact size is non-negotiable

  • Passport and visa photos — governments specify an exact size (commonly 2×2 inches for US passports) with strict tolerances, and photos outside that range get rejected outright.
  • ID badges and access cards — the printing system usually expects one fixed pixel size and will stretch or crop unpredictably if you feed it anything else.
  • Print lab templates — ordering a 4×6 print means the file needs to match that ratio and resolution, or the lab crops it for you in a way you didn't choose.
  • Website and app assets — a hero image slot or a profile picture upload area often expects one fixed size, and anything else gets awkwardly stretched or cropped by the platform.

Why "close enough" causes problems

When you upload a photo that's the right shape but the wrong exact size, one of two things usually happens: the receiving system stretches it to fit (distorting faces and straight lines) or crops it further on its own terms, often cutting off exactly the part you cared about keeping. Neither is what you wanted. Getting the exact size right yourself, before uploading, means you control what stays in frame — not an automated system guessing on your behalf.

How to crop to an exact size

The reliable way is a crop tool that lets you type in a target width and height directly, rather than dragging a selection box freehand and hoping it lands close. You position the crop area over the part of the photo you want to keep, lock in the exact pixel dimensions you were given, and export — no guessing, no manual measuring against a ruler on your screen.

A quick note on resolution

Exact pixel size and exact print size aren't interchangeable without knowing the resolution. A 600×600 pixel crop is a fixed number of pixels regardless of context, but if a print lab asks for "2×2 inches," that only becomes a real pixel target once you know the DPI they expect — at 300 DPI, 2×2 inches works out to exactly 600×600 pixels. When a spec gives you both a physical size and a DPI, do the multiplication first so you're cropping to the right pixel count, not just something that looks roughly 2 inches on your own screen.

What to do when the source photo isn't the same shape as the target

Often the photo you have doesn't naturally match the ratio you need — a wide landscape shot when you need a square, or a portrait shot when you need a wide banner. In that case, cropping necessarily means losing some of the original frame; there's no way around it without distorting the image. The only real decision is what to keep. Position the crop box over the part of the photo that actually matters — a face, a product, the focal point — rather than defaulting to a center crop, which can just as easily cut off exactly what you wanted kept.

Quick answers

What if my photo is smaller than the exact size I need? Cropping only removes pixels, it can't add detail that isn't there — if your source photo is too small for the target size, you'd need to upscale it first, which comes with its own quality trade-offs, or start from a higher-resolution original.

Does cropping to an exact size distort the photo? Not if you crop rather than resize-to-fit. Cropping removes parts of the image while keeping everything else at its original proportions; only stretching an image to fill a shape it doesn't naturally match causes distortion.

Can I crop to an exact size and keep it centered on a specific part of the photo? Yes — a good crop tool lets you position the crop box freely before locking the dimensions, so you choose exactly what stays in frame rather than always cropping from the center.
