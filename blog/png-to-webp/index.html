PNG to WebP: How Much Smaller Do Your Images Get?

If you've ever wondered how much space you'd actually save converting a PNG to WebP, the honest answer is “it depends” — but with real numbers behind it, not a vague guess.

What is WebP, quickly

WebP is a modern image format built by Google that can do everything PNG does — including lossless compression and full transparency support — but generally squeezes the same pixels into a smaller file. It can also do lossy compression, similar to JPG, for even bigger savings when you don't need every pixel preserved exactly.

How much smaller does a PNG actually get as WebP?

Converted losslessly — meaning every pixel stays identical, nothing is discarded — a PNG typically shrinks by around 20-30% as WebP. A 500KB PNG screenshot might come out around 350-400KB, with zero visible or measurable difference. Convert the same file to lossy WebP instead, and the savings jump dramatically: that same 500KB PNG can often drop under 100KB at a quality setting where the difference is still not noticeable to the eye. The gap is even bigger for PNGs of photographs, since PNG's lossless compression was never well suited to photographic detail in the first place.

Does WebP keep the transparency?

Yes — this is one of WebP's biggest advantages over JPG. WebP supports a full alpha channel, exactly like PNG, so a transparent PNG logo or graphic converts to WebP with its transparency fully intact. JPG can't do this at all, which is why WebP (not JPG) is the real modern alternative to PNG for anything that needs a see-through background.

When PNG still wins despite the size cost

  • Guaranteed universal compatibility. PNG is supported absolutely everywhere, including older software and specific print or design pipelines that may not accept WebP.
  • A workflow built around PNG. If downstream tools, a CMS, or a print process specifically expects PNG files, converting adds a step rather than saving one.
  • You need a format nobody will ever question. WebP has excellent support now, but PNG has zero compatibility risk anywhere, on anything, ever.

When it's worth converting

The clearest case is website performance — smaller image files mean faster page loads, and that adds up fast across a site with dozens of images. It's also worth it when you're managing a large asset library and storage space matters, or when you need to share a big batch of PNG graphics somewhere with a size limit. For a single one-off PNG you're using once, the savings usually aren't worth the extra step — it's when the same choice repeats hundreds or thousands of times that the size difference actually shows up.

A concrete example

Say you run a site with 40 product photos saved as PNG because the original files had transparent backgrounds, averaging 600KB each — that's roughly 24MB of image data for one page. Converted to lossless WebP, the same set drops to somewhere around 17-19MB, with every pixel identical. Allow lossy WebP instead, and the same 24MB could realistically shrink to 6-8MB, a difference a visitor on a slow connection will actually feel. Multiply that by every page on a catalog site and the total bandwidth saved becomes substantial.

Converting PNG to WebP

Converting is a quick, one-way operation — upload or drag in your PNG files, and get back WebP versions at a fraction of the size, transparency preserved. If you later need to go back the other way (say, a design tool that only accepts PNG), that conversion is just as quick, though be aware you're re-encoding rather than perfectly reconstructing any detail a lossy pass may have discarded.

Quick answers

Will converting PNG to WebP always shrink the file? Almost always, yes — even lossless WebP compression tends to outperform PNG's, though the size drop is much larger if you allow lossy compression.

Is lossy WebP noticeably worse looking than PNG? At a reasonable quality setting, no — the difference is generally invisible at normal viewing sizes, similar to how JPG compression works on photos.

Should I convert every PNG on my website to WebP? For images used repeatedly or in large numbers, it's usually worth it. For a handful of small icons, the savings are real but marginal.
