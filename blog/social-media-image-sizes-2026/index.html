The Right Image Size for Every Social Media Platform (2026)

Every platform has its own idea of the "right" image size, and they don't agree with each other. Post the wrong dimensions and a platform will crop, letterbox, or stretch your image on its own terms — usually not in your favor. Here's a current reference for getting it right the first time.

Why this keeps changing

Social platforms adjust their recommended sizes as they redesign feeds, add new placements (stories, reels, banners), and change how images render on newer, higher-resolution phone screens. A size that was correct two years ago can now display slightly cropped or padded with empty space, because the platform quietly updated its display container without announcing it loudly. Checking current numbers before an important post — a launch, a profile refresh, an ad campaign — is worth the extra minute it takes.

Treat these as current best targets rather than absolute law — platforms round differently and occasionally shift a few pixels, but staying close to these numbers keeps your image away from the crop lines a platform applies to anything wildly off-spec. When a platform doesn't publish an exact number for a specific placement, matching the closest listed size is a safer bet than guessing at something entirely new.

The "safe area" trap on banners

Wide banner formats — LinkedIn banners, YouTube channel art, Facebook covers — often get partially covered by a profile photo, buttons, or UI elements depending on whether someone's viewing on desktop or mobile. That's why YouTube specifically publishes a smaller "safe area" inside its 2560×1440 banner: anything important (a logo, text, a face) needs to sit inside that safe zone, or it risks being cropped off entirely on some devices even though the full image technically uploaded fine.

Square vs portrait vs landscape — pick based on where it lives

The same photo often needs different crops for different placements, not just different sizes. A landscape photo that works great as a YouTube thumbnail (16:9) will lose its subject entirely if force-fit into an Instagram Story's tall 9:16 frame without recropping first. Rather than uploading one crop everywhere and letting each platform mangle it differently, cropping a version specifically for each placement keeps the composition intentional instead of accidental — a subject centered perfectly for a square post can end up awkwardly off to one side once the same file is force-fit into a much taller or wider frame.

How to hit these sizes without guessing

A crop tool built around common social presets saves you from calculating aspect ratios by hand. Rather than eyeballing a drag-selection and hoping it's close to 1080×1080, you pick the platform and placement, and the tool constrains the crop to exactly that ratio and size — so what you export matches what the platform actually expects, not an approximation.

Planning ahead for a batch of posts

If you're preparing several images at once — a week's worth of posts, a campaign launch, a rebrand across profiles — it's faster to crop everything to spec before you start scheduling than to fix rejected or awkwardly-cropped uploads one at a time afterward. Build a habit of checking the target size for each placement first, then crop directly to that size, rather than uploading a single one-size-fits-all image and hoping each platform handles it gracefully. It rarely does.

Quick answers

What happens if I upload the wrong size? Most platforms will auto-crop or letterbox (add padding) rather than reject the upload outright — but you lose control over exactly what gets cut off or how the padding looks, which is why matching the spec yourself is worth doing.

Do these sizes matter for image quality, or just layout? Both — using a size well below the platform's target can result in a blurry or upscaled-looking image once it renders in the feed, since the platform stretches a too-small file to fill its display container.

Should I check for updates to these numbers periodically? Yes, especially before a major campaign — platforms update dimensions occasionally as they redesign, and a size that was standard a year or two ago can now display slightly off.
