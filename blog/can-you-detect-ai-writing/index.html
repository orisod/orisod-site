Why "AI Detectors" Can't Actually Detect AI Writing

A tool that claims to tell you, with a confident percentage, whether a piece of text was written by AI sounds like exactly the kind of thing that should be possible. It isn't — not because the engineering is immature, but because of what large language models are actually trained to do in the first place.

What a language model is optimized to do

A language model is trained to predict plausible next words given everything written so far, using patterns learned from enormous amounts of human-written text. Its entire objective is to produce output that's statistically consistent with how humans write — not to leave behind some distinctive fingerprint that marks it as machine-generated. There's no equivalent of a watermark baked into the writing itself unless a system deliberately adds one on purpose (some do, as an opt-in, separate feature) — by default, a model's whole job is to blend in with the distribution of text it learned from.

Why detectors end up guessing at style, not certainty

Without a built-in signature to look for, a detector is left inferring authorship from surface statistics: how predictable each word choice is given the words before it, how much sentence structure varies, how often certain phrasings show up. These are genuinely correlated with AI-generated text in aggregate — but they're statistical tendencies, not fixed rules, and human writing spans the same range. A careful, methodical human writer can produce highly predictable, low-variation prose; a language model prompted with unusual instructions can produce erratic, high-variation prose. The two distributions overlap substantially, and a detector has to draw a hard line somewhere inside that overlap.

Real, documented failure modes

This isn't a hypothetical concern — it shows up in practice in well-documented ways. Detectors have been shown to disproportionately flag writing by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, likely because that writing shares statistical properties (simpler sentence structure, more predictable word choices) with model output for reasons that have nothing to do with authorship. Detectors also routinely fail against text that's been lightly edited after AI generation, or against newer models they weren't calibrated against, since the underlying statistical signature shifts every time model architectures and training data change. A tool tuned to catch one generation of models can miss the next one entirely.

What a heuristic style-pattern checker is actually for

Given all that, a more honest tool doesn't try to output a probability of AI authorship at all — it flags specific, nameable patterns that show up often in generic writing (overused stock phrases, repetitive sentence openers, unusually uniform sentence rhythm) and lets a person decide what those patterns mean in context. This is a narrower, more defensible claim: "this phrase appears in the text" is simply true or false, verifiable by anyone reading the flagged span, unlike "this text is 73% likely to be AI-generated," which asserts a precision the underlying method can't actually support.

Quick answers

Does this mean AI detectors are useless? Not entirely — they can be one weak signal among several in a larger judgment call, especially in aggregate across many samples. The problem is treating a single score on a single piece of text as a reliable verdict, which the false-positive and false-negative rates don't support.

Can writers avoid every flagged pattern to "beat" a checker? Some of these patterns can be edited out deliberately, but that's the wrong goal — the patterns exist because they're genuinely common in weak or formulaic writing, human or AI. Removing them because they're flagged, rather than because the writing genuinely improves, optimizes for the wrong thing.

Why do stock phrases show up so often in AI writing in the first place? These phrases are common across huge volumes of formal and semi-formal writing that models are trained on, so they're statistically "safe," high-probability choices for a model trying to produce fluent, generically appropriate text — the same reason they show up constantly in human corporate writing, marketing copy, and school essays too.
