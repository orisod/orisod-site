WebP vs JPG: Why Your Images Won't Open (and How to Fix It)

You right-click and save an image from a website, and instead of the familiar .jpg you get a .webp file that half your software refuses to open. Here's what WebP actually is, why it's become the web's default, and the quickest way to get a JPG back.

What is WebP?

WebP is an image format developed by Google specifically for the web. It can do everything JPG does — lossy compression for photos — but more efficiently, typically producing files 25-35% smaller than an equivalent-quality JPG. It can also do lossless compression like PNG, and it supports transparency, which JPG never has. In short, it's a genuinely more capable format, purpose-built for how images actually get used online. A 2MB JPG photo, for example, might come out around 1.3MB as a WebP at the same visual quality — a meaningful saving once you multiply it across every image on a page.

Why so many websites use WebP now

Smaller image files mean faster-loading pages, and page speed affects everything from user experience to search rankings. Once major browsers added native WebP support, websites had a clear incentive to switch their images over — same visual quality, meaningfully less data to download. Most modern websites, content management systems, and even social platforms now serve images as WebP by default, often without the average visitor ever noticing the format has changed. From the website's perspective, there's essentially no downside — visitors get a faster page, and the format swap happens automatically behind the scenes.

Where WebP compatibility breaks down

The catch is that "the web" and "every piece of software on your computer" aren't the same thing. Plenty of image editors, older design tools, some printing services, and certain upload forms still don't recognize .webp files, especially software that hasn't been updated in a while. When that happens you'll see a generic "unsupported format" error, a broken image icon, or the upload simply gets rejected outright — the file itself isn't broken, the receiving software just doesn't have a WebP decoder.

Why you often can't avoid downloading WebP

This isn't usually something you chose. When you right-click and save an image from a website, your browser downloads whatever format the site actually served — and increasingly, that's WebP, regardless of what format the site originally uploaded. You don't get a say in the matter until after the file is already on your device, at which point converting it is the only practical option if the software you need it for doesn't cooperate.

Does converting WebP to JPG lose quality?

There's a small re-encoding step involved, same as converting between any two compressed formats, but at a reasonable quality setting it's not something you'll see with your eyes. The more noticeable change is usually file size increasing slightly, since JPG's compression is less efficient than WebP's — a fair trade for a file that will actually open in whatever you're using it for.

Converting WebP to JPG

When you need a WebP image to work in software or a form that only understands JPG, converting is a one-way trip that takes a few seconds — decode the WebP, re-encode it as a standard JPG, and you're left with a file that opens absolutely anywhere. This is especially common after saving images from websites, since the format you downloaded was chosen for the website's performance, not for compatibility with whatever you're about to use it in.

Quick answers

Is WebP a "worse" format than JPG? No — it's technically better in almost every way. The only real downside is inconsistent support outside web browsers.

Will converting WebP to JPG make the file bigger? Usually yes, since JPG is the less efficient of the two formats. That's an expected trade-off for gaining universal compatibility.

Can I avoid this problem by just using JPG everywhere? For your own files, sure — but you can't control what format a website chooses to serve, so you'll keep running into WebP images you didn't choose.
